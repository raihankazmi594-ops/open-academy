Independent undergraduate-style programme

Twenty-four weeks
of learning to think.

A structured route through philosophical history, ethics, politics, existentialism, religion, mind and Eastern traditions. Designed for 30 minutes a day, with primary-text reading, written work and regular assessment.

Academic note. This is a demanding self-study curriculum inspired by undergraduate philosophy teaching, not an accredited university degree or formal qualification. It is intentionally selective: read short, carefully chosen sections of primary texts, then analyse them rather than trying to finish every book.

How to study each week

Use six short sessions and keep one day flexible. On reading days, annotate the author's conclusion, premises, key terms and one objection. If a text is difficult, reread a short passage rather than racing ahead.

Term I · Weeks 1–6

Foundations of philosophy

Questions · Knowledge · Logic · Greece
Week 01 · Orientation

What is philosophy?

Philosophical questions, branches, arguments and the difference between opinion and justified belief.

Readings & tasks
  • Read: OpenStax, Introduction to Philosophy, opening chapter.
  • Read: Plato, Apology, Socrates' defence (selected sections).
  • Task: Write 300 words answering “What makes a question philosophical?”
  • Check: Define metaphysics, epistemology, ethics, logic and political philosophy.
Week 02 · Ancient Greece

Socrates, Plato and the examined life

Socratic questioning, virtue, knowledge, the Forms and the difference between appearance and reality.

Readings & tasks
  • Plato, Euthyphro (whole dialogue, if possible).
  • Plato, Republic, Book VII (Allegory of the Cave).
  • Task: Explain the Cave and give one modern analogy.
  • Check: Is Socrates seeking definitions, knowledge, or moral improvement?
Week 03 · Aristotle

Virtue, happiness and human flourishing

Aristotle's account of eudaimonia, virtue as a mean, habituation and practical wisdom.

Readings & tasks
  • Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics, Books I–II (selected chapters).
  • Task: Apply the doctrine of the mean to courage or generosity.
  • Write a 250-word explanation of why flourishing is more than pleasure.
Week 04 · Knowledge

Scepticism, reason and experience

Descartes' method of doubt, rationalism, empiricism and the limits of perception.

Readings & tasks
  • Descartes, Meditations, I–II.
  • Hume, An Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding, Sections II–IV (selected).
  • Task: Compare one rationalist and one empiricist claim.
Week 05 · Logic

How arguments work

Premises, conclusions, validity, soundness, deduction, induction and common fallacies.

Readings & tasks
  • Read: forallx: Calgary, introductory chapters on arguments and validity.
  • Task: Reconstruct three arguments from an opinion article.
  • Check: Distinguish validity from truth; identify an equivocation and a straw man.
Week 06 · Assessment I

Reasoning and ancient thought review

Consolidate the first term and practise a short university-style philosophical essay.

Assessment
  • Timed assessment: 30 minutes; define key terms, analyse a short argument and answer one conceptual question.
  • Essay (800–1,000 words): “Is the examined life necessary for living well?” Use Plato and one objection.
  • Self-review using the essay rubric below.
Term II · Weeks 7–12

Ethics, politics and society

Morality · Justice · Freedom · Power
Week 07 · Virtue ethics

What makes a good person?

Aristotle, character, moral education, practical wisdom and the role of community.

Readings & tasks
  • Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics, Books II and VI (selections).
  • Task: Analyse a difficult choice as a conflict between virtues.
  • Check: Explain why virtue is a stable disposition, not a single action.
Week 08 · Consequences

Utilitarianism and the greatest happiness

Bentham and Mill; impartiality, utility, higher and lower pleasures, and objections.

Readings & tasks
  • Mill, Utilitarianism, Chapters II and IV.
  • Task: Apply utilitarian reasoning to a real policy dilemma.
  • Objection: Can rights or justice be sacrificed for aggregate welfare?
Week 09 · Duties

Kant and moral obligation

Good will, duty, universalisation and treating persons as ends in themselves.

Readings & tasks
  • Kant, Groundwork of the Metaphysics of Morals, Sections I–II (selected passages).
  • Task: Test a maxim using the universal law formulation.
  • Compare Kant's approach with Mill's.
Week 10 · Political authority

Hobbes, Locke and the social contract

State of nature, consent, rights, security, property and legitimate government.

Readings & tasks
  • Hobbes, Leviathan, Chapters 13, 17–18.
  • Locke, Second Treatise, Chapters II–V (selected).
  • Task: Compare why each thinks political authority is justified.
Week 11 · Justice and liberty

Mill, Marx and Rawls

Freedom of expression, harm principle, class, equality and fairness.

Readings & tasks
  • Mill, On Liberty, Chapters I–II.
  • Marx and Engels, Communist Manifesto, Part I.
  • Rawls, A Theory of Justice, original position (reliable overview or excerpt).
  • Task: Defend or challenge the harm principle with an example.
Week 12 · Assessment II

Ethics and political philosophy essay

Bring moral theory and political legitimacy into conversation.

Assessment
  • Essay (1,000–1,200 words): “When, if ever, may the state restrict individual liberty?”
  • Use at least two thinkers from Mill, Hobbes, Locke, Marx or Rawls.
  • Include a clear thesis, strongest objection and reasoned reply.
  • Short quiz: ethical theories, social contract and justice.
Term III · Weeks 13–18

Existence, religion and human nature

Meaning · Faith · Mind · Identity
Week 13 · Existentialism

Freedom, choice and responsibility

Kierkegaard, Sartre and the idea that human beings must confront their freedom.

Readings & tasks
  • Sartre, Existentialism Is a Humanism (selected sections).
  • Kierkegaard, Fear and Trembling (short guided extract or scholarly overview).
  • Task: Explain “existence precedes essence” and challenge it.
Week 14 · Absurdity

Camus and the search for meaning

The absurd, revolt, authenticity and whether meaning must be discovered or created.

Readings & tasks
  • Camus, The Myth of Sisyphus, opening and “The Myth of Sisyphus” (selections).
  • Task: Is creating meaning enough to make life meaningful?
  • Write a dialogue between an existentialist and a sceptic.
Week 15 · Religion

Arguments for and against God

Cosmological, teleological and ontological arguments; evil, suffering and religious experience.

Readings & tasks
  • Read Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy entries on cosmological arguments and problem of evil.
  • Hume, Dialogues Concerning Natural Religion (selected sections).
  • Task: Reconstruct one argument and identify its most vulnerable premise.
Week 16 · Mind and self

Consciousness, identity and personal survival

Dualism, physicalism, the mind-body problem and what makes you the same person over time.

Readings & tasks
  • Read SEP entries: “Personal Identity” and “The Mind/Brain Identity Theory”.
  • Locke, Essay Concerning Human Understanding, Book II, Chapter XXVII (selected).
  • Task: Consider whether memory is necessary for personal identity.
Week 17 · Human nature

Reason, emotion and the unconscious

Plato's tripartite soul, Hume on reason and passion, and philosophical questions raised by psychology.

Readings & tasks
  • Plato, Republic, Book IV (parts on the soul).
  • Hume, Treatise of Human Nature, Book II (selected passages).
  • Task: Does reason govern emotion, or does emotion guide reason?
Week 18 · Assessment III

Meaning, mind and belief

Integrate existential, religious and philosophical psychology.

Assessment
  • Essay (1,000–1,200 words): “Can a meaningful life be lived without belief in God?”
  • Use two contrasting arguments and address a serious objection.
  • Short oral-style reflection: explain your position in three minutes (recording optional).
Term IV · Weeks 19–24

Eastern philosophy and advanced thought

Dao · Dharma · Emptiness · Synthesis
Week 19 · Confucian thought

Virtue, relationships and social harmony

Confucius on ren, li, moral cultivation, ritual, learning and good government.

Readings & tasks
  • Analects, Books 1, 2, 4 and 12 (selected passages).
  • Task: Compare Confucian virtue with Aristotle's account of character.
  • Question: Is morality primarily individual or relational?
Week 20 · Daoism

Laozi, Zhuangzi and the Dao

Naturalness, non-forcing (wu wei), language, perspective and living in accordance with the Dao.

Readings & tasks
  • Dao De Jing, Chapters 1, 2, 8, 37 and 48.
  • Zhuangzi, “The Butterfly Dream” and selected passages.
  • Task: Explain why wu wei is not simply doing nothing.
Week 21 · Buddhist philosophy

Suffering, impermanence and no-self

The Four Noble Truths, dependent origination, impermanence and the philosophical challenge of an enduring self.

Readings & tasks
  • Read selected passages from the Dhammacakkappavattana Sutta.
  • Read an introductory scholarly overview of Buddhist philosophy and no-self.
  • Task: Compare Buddhist no-self with Locke's account of personal identity.
Week 22 · Hindu philosophy

The Bhagavad Gita, self and duty

Dharma, action without attachment, the self and liberation; avoid treating Hindu traditions as a single uniform system.

Readings & tasks
  • Bhagavad Gita, Chapters 2, 3 and 12 (selected).
  • Task: Explain action without attachment and raise one ethical difficulty.
  • Compare duty in the Gita with Kantian duty.
Week 23 · Comparative philosophy

Can philosophical traditions speak to one another?

Compare methods, assumptions and concepts without flattening cultural differences.

Readings & tasks
  • Revisit one primary text from each of the four terms.
  • Read one scholarly comparative philosophy overview.
  • Task: Build a comparison matrix: self, knowledge, virtue, society and ultimate reality.
Week 24 · Final assessment

Your philosophical position

Demonstrate careful reading, independent argument, comparison and intellectual humility.

Capstone assessment
  • Final essay (1,500–2,000 words): choose one fundamental question and defend a clear answer using at least three philosophers, including one Eastern thinker.
  • Submit a 500-word reflective note: which view changed your thinking most, and why?
  • Complete a 45-minute cumulative assessment across all four terms (split into two sessions if needed).

Essay and assessment standards

Use this rubric for each major essay. Aim for clear reasoning, not impressive-sounding language. A strong essay answers a precise question and gives the reader reasons to accept its conclusion.

Thesis and focus · 20%
Direct answer to the question; scope is clear and sustained.
Understanding · 25%
Accurate explanation of primary texts and concepts; no straw-manning.
Argument · 30%
Reasons support the conclusion; objections are serious; replies are defensible.
Structure and style · 15%
Logical paragraphs, clear signposting and precise prose.
Evidence and citation · 10%
References to passages, consistent bibliography and honest attribution.
Suggested essay structure: Introduction and thesis → explain the central argument → evaluate its strongest reason → present an objection → reply to the objection → conclusion. Use a consistent citation style (Harvard or Chicago) and record page/section numbers as you read.
